What a Facility Fee Is

A facility fee is a separate charge that hospitals and hospital-owned outpatient departments add to the cost of a procedure, visit, or service. It is billed in addition to the physician's professional fee — meaning you receive two charges for what feels like one service.

When you visit an independent doctor's office for a routine consultation, you typically receive one bill. When you visit that same doctor for the same consultation at a hospital-owned outpatient clinic, you may receive two bills: one from the physician and one from the hospital for the use of the facility. The clinical service is identical. The billing is not.

Facility fees exist across nearly all types of outpatient hospital services — office visits, imaging, lab work, minor procedures, infusions, and surgical care. They are one of the least visible and most consequential factors in healthcare pricing.

Why They Exist

Hospitals argue that facility fees cover the operational costs of maintaining a hospital-grade environment. This includes building and equipment costs, 24/7 staffing requirements, emergency preparedness, regulatory compliance, infection control protocols, electronic health record systems, and administrative overhead.

These costs are real. Hospitals operate under significantly more regulatory burden than independent clinics. They maintain emergency departments, meet accreditation standards, staff specialists around the clock, and invest in infrastructure that independent clinics do not need.

The debate is not whether hospitals have higher costs — they do. The debate is whether those higher costs should apply to routine outpatient services that do not require hospital-level resources. When a simple office visit or routine MRI costs three times more at a hospital outpatient department than at an independent clinic, the facility fee is doing most of the work in that price difference.

The Price Difference in Practice

The impact of facility fees is not theoretical. For many common procedures, the price difference between a hospital outpatient department and an independent clinic is substantial.

Example: MRI (Brain, Without Contrast)
Independent imaging center $350 – $700
Hospital outpatient department $1,500 – $4,000
Typical facility fee portion $800 – $2,500
Example: Routine Office Visit (Established Patient)
Independent physician office $100 – $250
Hospital-owned outpatient clinic $250 – $600
Typical facility fee portion $100 – $350

These are representative ranges. Actual prices vary by location, provider, and insurance. The point is structural: the same clinical service, performed by the same type of physician, costs significantly more when a facility fee is applied.

How Facility Fees Are Billed

Facility fees appear on your bill as a separate line item, often described as a "facility charge," "hospital outpatient fee," or "technical component." In medical billing, services at hospital outpatient departments are typically billed using two claim forms: a CMS-1500 for the physician's professional fee and a UB-04 for the facility's institutional fee.

For patients, this means two separate charges, often from two separate billing departments, sometimes arriving weeks apart. The physician's bill may seem reasonable. The facility bill — which arrives separately — is where the cost multiplier appears.

This dual-billing structure is particularly confusing because patients who visit a hospital-owned clinic may not realize they are being treated at a hospital outpatient department. The clinic may look, feel, and operate exactly like an independent office. But if it is owned by a hospital system, it can bill as a hospital outpatient department and add a facility fee.

Who Pays Facility Fees

Facility fees affect different patients differently depending on their insurance status and plan design.

Insured patients with deductibles bear the most direct impact. If you have not met your deductible, you pay the full facility fee out of pocket. Even after meeting your deductible, your coinsurance applies to the facility fee — meaning a 20% coinsurance on a $2,000 facility fee is $400 that would not exist at an independent clinic.

Uninsured and self-pay patients may be charged the full facility fee at chargemaster rates, which are the highest published prices. Some hospitals offer self-pay discounts, but the base price including the facility fee is still typically much higher than an independent alternative.

Medicare patients are affected because Medicare pays different rates for the same service depending on the site of care. Hospital outpatient departments receive higher Medicare reimbursements than independent offices for many services, and the patient's copay reflects that difference.

Hospital Acquisitions and Facility Fees

One of the most significant — and least understood — drivers of facility fee growth is hospital acquisition of independent physician practices. When a hospital system acquires an independent clinic, the clinic can be reclassified as a hospital outpatient department. The same doctors, the same building, the same services — but now with a facility fee attached to every visit.

This practice has accelerated over the past decade. Research from health policy organizations has consistently found that prices increase after hospital acquisition of physician practices, with facility fees being a primary mechanism.

For patients, the change can be invisible. Your doctor may send a letter saying they have "joined" a health system. The office address does not change. The receptionist is the same. But your next bill includes a facility fee that did not exist before.

What to watch for
If your physician's practice is acquired by a hospital system, your costs for the same services may increase due to facility fees. Ask your doctor's office directly whether the acquisition changes how services are billed and whether a facility fee will now apply.

How to Avoid Facility Fees

Options for avoiding facility fees
1. Choose independent clinics, physician offices, or freestanding centers instead of hospital outpatient departments
2. For imaging (MRI, CT, X-ray), use freestanding imaging centers — pricing is typically 50-75% lower
3. For minor surgical procedures, ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs) generally do not charge hospital-level facility fees
4. Ask every provider before scheduling whether the location charges a facility fee
5. If your doctor has been acquired by a hospital system, ask whether the same services are available at a non-hospital location within the system
6. Compare pricing across facility types using real data before committing to a provider

Not all services can be performed outside of a hospital setting. Complex surgeries, emergency care, and procedures requiring hospital-level monitoring appropriately take place in hospitals. The facility fee question is most relevant for routine outpatient services — office visits, imaging, lab work, minor procedures, and elective surgeries — where independent alternatives exist.

Questions to Ask Before Scheduling

Before your appointment
— Is this location classified as a hospital outpatient department?
— Will I receive a separate facility charge in addition to the physician's fee?
— What is the estimated total cost including any facility fees?
— Is this same service available at a non-hospital location within your system?
— Can you provide a Good Faith Estimate that includes all expected charges?

These questions are not confrontational. They are the same questions insurance companies and employer benefits teams ask when evaluating provider networks. Asking them as a patient is a reasonable step in understanding what you will be charged.

Transparency Laws and Facility Fees

Awareness of facility fees has driven legislative action at both state and federal levels. Several states have enacted or proposed laws requiring providers to disclose facility fees to patients before treatment. These laws vary in scope and enforcement but share a common principle: patients should know about facility fees before they incur them, not after.

At the federal level, the No Surprises Act requires Good Faith Estimates for uninsured and self-pay patients, which must include facility fees as part of the total estimated cost. Hospital price transparency rules also require hospitals to publish machine-readable pricing files that include facility charges.

Despite these requirements, compliance and clarity remain inconsistent. Many patients still discover facility fees only when the bill arrives. Proactive comparison of pricing across facility types remains the most reliable way to understand and manage facility fee exposure.

Limitations

This guide describes general facility fee practices in the United States as of May 2026. Billing practices, regulations, and transparency requirements vary significantly by state. Some states have specific facility fee disclosure laws while others do not.

The price ranges cited are representative and based on publicly available data. Actual costs depend on the specific provider, location, insurance plan, and clinical circumstances. Facility fees are not inherently inappropriate — hospitals do have higher operational costs — but the scale of the price difference for routine services is a legitimate concern for patients and policymakers.
