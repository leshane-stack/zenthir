Your Rights Under the No Surprises Act

What the No Surprises Act Is

The No Surprises Act is a federal law that took effect on January 1, 2022. It established protections against unexpected medical bills — commonly called "surprise bills" — that occur when patients receive care from out-of-network providers without their knowledge or consent.

Before this law, patients who went to an in-network hospital could still receive large bills from individual providers within that hospital — an anesthesiologist, radiologist, or pathologist — who happened to be out of network. Patients had no way to know this in advance and no way to choose an alternative. The No Surprises Act addresses this by shifting billing disputes between providers and insurers, rather than placing the burden on patients.

Who It Applies To

The No Surprises Act applies to most people with private health insurance, including employer-sponsored plans (both fully insured and self-insured), individual marketplace plans, and federal employee health plans.

The law also provides specific protections for uninsured and self-pay patients, primarily through the Good Faith Estimate requirement.

The law does not apply to Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, VA health benefits, or Indian Health Service coverage. These programs have their own billing protections and dispute processes. If you are covered by one of these programs, your protections come from different federal rules, not the No Surprises Act.

Emergency Care Protections

When you receive emergency care, the No Surprises Act prohibits out-of-network providers and facilities from billing you more than your in-network cost-sharing amount. This applies regardless of whether the emergency room, hospital, or individual providers are in your insurance network.

This protection covers the emergency service itself and any care you receive after being stabilized until you can be safely transferred or give informed consent to continued out-of-network care. The key principle is that patients seeking emergency care cannot reasonably choose their providers, so they should not bear the cost of out-of-network billing.

Your insurer is required to apply in-network cost-sharing rates (deductible, copay, coinsurance) to these emergency services, and these amounts must count toward your in-network deductible and out-of-pocket maximum.

In-Network Facility Protections

When you receive care at an in-network facility — such as a hospital, surgical center, or outpatient clinic — certain out-of-network providers who treat you at that facility cannot send you surprise bills. This commonly applies to anesthesiologists, pathologists, radiologists, assistant surgeons, neonatologists, and hospitalists.

If you schedule a procedure at an in-network hospital, you should not receive a separate surprise bill from an out-of-network anesthesiologist who was assigned to your case without your input. Instead, the billing dispute is resolved between the provider and your insurer.

There is an important exception: if a provider gives you written notice at least 72 hours before the procedure (or at least 3 hours before in certain circumstances) that they are out of network, and you sign a consent form agreeing to out-of-network care, the surprise billing protections may not apply. Be cautious about signing such forms, especially under time pressure before a procedure.

Good Faith Estimates

The No Surprises Act requires healthcare providers and facilities to give uninsured and self-pay patients a written Good Faith Estimate of expected charges before scheduled care. This estimate must be provided when you schedule a service at least 3 business days in advance, or within 1 business day for services scheduled within 3 days.

A Good Faith Estimate should include expected charges for the primary service and any reasonably expected associated services — such as facility fees, anesthesia, imaging, lab work, and follow-up care. The estimate must identify each provider and their expected charges.

If the final bill exceeds the Good Faith Estimate by $400 or more, you have the right to dispute the charges through a patient-provider dispute resolution process. This is a significant consumer protection because it creates accountability around the accuracy of cost estimates.

Comparing Good Faith Estimates
A Good Faith Estimate tells you what one provider expects to charge. To understand whether that estimate is reasonable, it helps to compare it against what other providers in your area charge for the same procedure. Common Surprise Billing Scenarios

Understanding where surprise bills typically occur helps patients recognize when protections may apply.

Anesthesiologist at an in-network hospital
You schedule surgery at an in-network hospital. The anesthesiologist assigned to your case is out of network. Under the No Surprises Act, that anesthesiologist generally cannot bill you beyond your in-network cost-sharing amount.
Emergency room physician
You visit the nearest emergency room during a medical emergency. The ER physicians are contracted independently and are out of network with your plan. The No Surprises Act protects you from balance billing in this scenario.
Radiologist reading your imaging
You get an MRI at an in-network imaging center. The radiologist who interprets the images is out of network. This is a common source of surprise bills that the law now addresses.
Pathologist after a biopsy or surgery
Tissue samples are sent to a pathology lab. The pathologist is out of network. You had no ability to choose or even know who the pathologist would be. The No Surprises Act covers this situation when it occurs at an in-network facility.
Air ambulance
You are transported by an out-of-network air ambulance service. The No Surprises Act prohibits balance billing for air ambulance services, though ground ambulance is excluded.

What the Law Does Not Cover

The No Surprises Act has meaningful limitations that are important to understand.

Ground ambulances are not covered. Ground ambulance billing remains one of the most common sources of surprise medical bills, and federal law does not yet address it. Some states have their own ground ambulance billing protections.

Out-of-network care you explicitly consent to is generally not covered. If you are informed in advance that a provider is out of network and you sign a written consent form waiving your surprise billing protections, the law may not protect you from the resulting charges.

Short-term, limited-duration insurance plans are not covered. These plans are not considered comprehensive health coverage under federal law and are not subject to No Surprises Act protections.

Medicare, Medicaid, and government programs have separate billing protections and are not governed by this law.

What to Do If You Receive a Surprise Bill

Steps to take
1. Request an itemized bill. Ask the provider for a complete bill showing every charge, service date, and billing codes used.
2. Check whether the No Surprises Act applies. Was it emergency care? Was the facility in-network? Did you sign a consent waiver? Is your plan covered by the law?
3. Contact your insurer. Call the number on your insurance card and ask whether the services should have been covered at in-network rates. Request reprocessing if applicable.
4. Do not ignore the bill. Unpaid medical bills can be sent to collections and affect your credit. Engage with the billing process while you dispute.
5. Do not pay a bill you believe is incorrect. Paying in full can make recovery harder. If concerned about collections, communicate in writing that you are disputing the charges.

What to Save Before Disputing a Bill

If you plan to dispute a surprise bill, gather and preserve the following documentation before initiating any dispute process.

Documentation checklist
— Your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) from your insurer
— The itemized bill with every line item and billing code
— Any Good Faith Estimate you received before the service
— Records of communication with provider or insurer (dates, names, details)
— Any consent forms you signed or were asked to sign
— Screenshots of your insurance portal showing network status at time of service

Having complete documentation significantly strengthens a dispute. Missing any of these items can slow the process or weaken your position.

How to File a Complaint

If you believe the No Surprises Act has been violated, you can file a complaint with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) at 1-800-985-3059 or through the CMS No Surprises Help Desk. You can also file with your state insurance department, which may have additional state-level protections.

When filing a complaint, include your itemized bill, EOB, any Good Faith Estimate, and a clear description of why you believe the billing violates the law.

Independent Dispute Resolution

The No Surprises Act established an Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR) process for resolving payment disagreements between providers and insurers. In most cases, this process happens between the provider and the insurer — the patient is not directly involved and should not be billed the disputed amount while the process is ongoing.

For uninsured or self-pay patients who received a Good Faith Estimate, a separate patient-provider dispute resolution process exists. If your final bill exceeds the Good Faith Estimate by $400 or more, you can initiate this process within 120 calendar days of receiving the bill. A dispute resolution entity will review the case and make a determination.

Limitations

This guide covers federal protections under the No Surprises Act. Many states have their own surprise billing laws that may provide broader protections. State laws vary significantly — some cover ground ambulances, some extend protections to state-regulated plans that federal law does not cover, and some have different dispute resolution processes. Check your state insurance department for state-specific protections.

The No Surprises Act is subject to ongoing rulemaking and enforcement updates by CMS. Provisions may change as regulations are finalized and court challenges are resolved. This guide reflects the law as understood in May 2026.

Nothing in this guide constitutes legal or medical advice. Individual situations vary, and patients with specific billing disputes should consult a qualified professional.
