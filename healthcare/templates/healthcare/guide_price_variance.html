Why Healthcare Prices Vary So Much

PUBLISHED MAY 2026 · 12 MIN READ · ZENTHIR HEALTHCARE INTELLIGENCE

Across 6 million pricing records from 654,657 providers, we found that the same medical procedure routinely costs 3-10x more at one provider than another — often in the same city. A routine office visit ranges from $137 to $506 (5th to 95th percentile), while a brain MRI ranges from $186 to $1,749. This analysis examines what drives that variance.

Why Do Healthcare Prices Vary?
1. Facility type — hospital outpatient departments add facility fees that independent clinics do not charge
2. Geography — coastal metros price 20-45% above the national average
3. Billing structure — submitted charges are 2-5x what insurance actually pays
4. Provider specialty — different practice types have different overhead structures
5. Market concentration — fewer competitors means higher prices

Facility Type: The Biggest Driver

One of the most important factors in what a healthcare service costs is where it is performed. Different types of facilities have fundamentally different cost structures and billing models, which directly affects what patients pay.

However, comparing averages across facility types requires care. A hospital's average charge includes bundled inpatient stays (such as a $80,000 sepsis treatment), while a family medicine practice's average reflects routine office visits. These are not equivalent comparisons. The more meaningful comparison is what different facility types charge for the same procedure.

The primary reason hospitals charge more is the facility fee — a separate charge added on top of the physician's professional fee. When you visit a hospital outpatient department for a routine office visit, you receive two bills: one for the physician and one for the facility itself. An independent clinic sends one bill.

For a brain MRI, the 5th percentile price is $186 and the 95th percentile is $1,749 — a 9.4x difference. The median is $320. Much of this range reflects whether the charge includes just the professional interpretation fee or the full technical and professional service combined.

Geographic Variance

After facility type, where you live is the second-largest factor in what you pay. A standard office visit (established patient, moderate complexity) averages $127 in Searcy, Arkansas and $626 in New Brunswick, New Jersey — a 5x difference for the identical service.

The pattern is consistent: coastal metropolitan areas — particularly the New York tri-state area, Southern California, and parts of New England — price 20-45% above the national average. The South and Midwest consistently price below.

This is driven by cost of living, market concentration (fewer providers means less price competition), and regional differences in how aggressively providers negotiate with insurance companies. In highly consolidated markets where one hospital system dominates, prices tend to be higher across the board.

What Providers Charge vs. What Gets Paid

One of the most misunderstood aspects of healthcare pricing is the gap between what providers submit as their charge and what insurance companies or Medicare actually pay. These are not the same number — and the gap is enormous.

Submitted Charge vs. Medicare Payment
Office Visit (Established, Moderate) $278 submitted → $119 paid (2.3x)
ECG / EKG (12-Lead) $64 submitted → $13 paid (4.8x)
Colonoscopy $3,152 submitted → $590 paid (5.3x)

For a colonoscopy, providers submit an average of $3,152 — but Medicare pays $590. That is a 5.3x gap. For an EKG, the gap is 4.8x.

This matters because uninsured and self-pay patients are often billed at the submitted charge rate — the highest price in the system. Many providers offer cash-pay discounts, but patients who do not ask may pay the full submitted amount while their insured neighbor pays a fraction of that for the identical service.

What you can do
If you are uninsured or self-pay, always ask for the cash-pay or self-pay rate before agreeing to a procedure. Many providers will reduce the price by 30-60% for patients who pay directly. You also have the right to request a Good Faith Estimate before any scheduled service.

Provider Specialty and Pricing

Beyond facility type and geography, provider specialty influences pricing — sometimes in counterintuitive ways. Different types of providers bill at different rates even for the same procedure, reflecting different practice overhead structures and billing patterns.

For example, a brain MRI averages $472 at a diagnostic radiology practice but $1,912 at an imaging center. This seems backwards until you understand the data: diagnostic radiology practices in our dataset are largely billing for the professional interpretation (the radiologist's reading fee), while imaging centers are billing for the full technical and professional service combined.

This distinction — between the professional component (the physician's fee for interpretation) and the technical component (the facility's fee for performing the scan) — is one of the most important and least understood aspects of medical billing. A single procedure can generate two separate bills from two separate entities, and comparing them requires understanding which components are included.

Why This Matters for Patients

Healthcare is the only industry where the same service performed on the same equipment by similarly qualified professionals can vary in price by an order of magnitude — and where the consumer often does not know the price until after the service is complete.

The consequences are real. A patient who chooses a hospital outpatient MRI at $1,082 instead of a diagnostic radiology center MRI at $472 pays $610 more — for the same scan. Over the course of a year of treatment involving multiple imaging studies, lab work, and specialist visits, these facility-type differences can add up to thousands of dollars.

The patients most affected are the uninsured, the underinsured, and those with high-deductible health plans — people who pay out of pocket for some or all of their care. For these patients, understanding pricing variance is not academic. It is the difference between affordable care and financial hardship.

For Patients: What This Means For You

Price variance in healthcare is structural — it will not be resolved by any single policy change or market force. But individual patients can make better decisions with better information.

Methodology

This analysis is based on 6,037,722 pricing records from CMS Medicare Provider Charge Data 2024, covering 654,657 providers across all 50 states and 36 specialties. Submitted charges represent what providers bill to Medicare — not what patients pay out of pocket. Medicare payments represent the allowed amount Medicare considers reasonable for each service. Cash prices for self-pay patients may differ. Provider type averages use physician-level charge data (CMS Medicare Physician Data) to ensure comparable procedure types. Hospital inpatient DRG-based charges are excluded from facility type comparisons as they represent bundled multi-day stays rather than individual procedures. Geographic comparisons use city-level averages for procedures with 50 or more records in that city. Full methodology available at zenthir.com/methodology.
